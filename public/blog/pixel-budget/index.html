A 3D room on a laptop with no graphics card

My portfolio's first 3D room took 95 ms to draw one frame on my own laptop. Here's how it got to 6.7 ms, and why every visitor now gets a pixel budget instead of a resolution.

01

The homepage of this site is a 3D room: a desk, a corkboard, a drawer of projects, a diary. I built it on the only computer I have, a Ryzen 5 7430U laptop with AMD Radeon integrated graphics and 16 GB of RAM. No graphics card.

02

The first version ran at about 11 frames a second on that laptop. At 1440×900 one frame took 95 ms to draw; at 1080p, 109 ms. On a phone with its CPU throttled 4× it was the same 95 ms. Under the hood it was making 2,156 draw calls for one view of the room, across 589 meshes.

"Frame time grew almost exactly with pixel count. So I stopped picking a resolution and started handing out a budget."

//

What was actually slow

03

Draw calls first. The 3D models I used are built from dozens of tiny parts. The keyboard alone was 190 separate meshes, and every part got drawn once for the picture and again for every shadow. The fix was to merge everything that never moves into one mesh per material when the room loads, and to drop the keyboard's 60 per-key legend materials. Draw calls went from 2,156 to about 300–450, meshes from 589 to 221.

04

The single biggest phone win was one material. The phone on the desk had a glass camera lens using transmission, which quietly forces a full second render of the whole scene every frame, and with tone mapping on it rebuilt shader programs every frame too. That was 30% of the CPU on phones. Swapping it for plain transparency took a phone frame from 43 ms to 20 ms.

05

Then the room stopped drawing when nothing changes. It renders at full rate only while something moves (a scroll, the pointer, the camera easing, a drawer sliding) and otherwise idles at 24–30 fps, just enough to keep the steam and dust alive. Shadows became static: they re-render only when something that casts one moves. The desk lamp used to sway gently when idle; I removed that, because it forced the shadows to re-render constantly.

06

Small things added up too. The phone's screen was a 900×1860 texture uploaded to the GPU every frame. Now it redraws once a minute, which is how often a clock needs to change.

//

A pixel budget, not a resolution

07

Once draw calls were down, cost was all about fill rate: frame time scaled almost linearly with the number of pixels drawn. So instead of a fixed resolution, each quality tier gets a budget in megapixels, and the render resolution is worked out from the visitor's screen: the square root of budget ÷ (width × height), kept between 0.6 and the device's own pixel ratio. A retina phone stays sharp enough. A 4K monitor on integrated graphics doesn't melt.

08

There are four tiers. High is for proper graphics cards and Apple silicon: ambient occlusion, bloom, anti-aliasing, 2k shadows, up to 3.6 megapixels. Mid is for laptops like mine: bloom and anti-aliasing, no ambient occlusion, 1k shadows, 1.6 megapixels. Low is for phones and machines with 4 GB of RAM or 4 cores or fewer: no post-processing, no dust, 0.9 megapixels. Poster is for devices with no WebGL2, data saver on, 2 GB of RAM or less, or a software GPU: they get a still image of the room with every section one tap away, and never download the 3D at all.

09

The first guess comes from the GPU name, RAM, cores, touch and screen. After the intro, a short frame-rate probe steps down one tier if the device can't hold it, and the result is remembered for next time. You can force any tier yourself: here is the room on the low tier.

//

Where it landed

10

On my laptop at 1440×900: 95 ms per frame before, 6.7 ms now on mid. At 1080p: 109 ms before, 7.8 ms on mid and 10.9 ms on high. A phone with its CPU throttled 4×: 95 ms before, 17 ms now on low, about 58 frames a second. A budget phone at 6× throttling: 29 ms, about 35 frames a second. One honest caveat: a phone's GPU can't be emulated on a laptop, so the phone numbers are realistic for the CPU and optimistic for the GPU.

//

What broke after it was fast

11

Fast in a benchmark isn't the same as fast for a visitor. Three things went wrong after the numbers looked good.

12

Laptops were mistaken for phones. Device detection leaned on screen size, and plenty of 1366×768 laptops looked like phones to it. Now phones are recognised by their primary input being touch, not by how small the screen is.

13

Phones froze mid-scroll. Shaders were compiling the first time each object came into view, which on a phone meant stutter halfway through the tour, and at worst a 12-second freeze. Now the room is built in stages and every shader is compiled in the background before the loading screen lifts.

14

And sometimes the screen just went black. Browsers can take the GPU away from a page mid-visit. When that happens now, the room shows a still image of itself and quietly rebuilds the 3D behind it.

"The slowest machines don't get a worse room. They get a different door: a still picture, or the terminal."

15

For machines that can't run it at all, the room isn't the only way in. They're offered the text terminal version of the site first, which has every project, role and essay, and loads instantly.

16

If you want to see the cost for yourself, open the room in roam mode. You can fly around the whole setup, and an inspector shows the frame rate, draw calls, triangles and render resolution live, with a switch for every tier.
